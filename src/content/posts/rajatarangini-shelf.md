The oldest surviving history of Kashmir is a 12th-century Sanskrit chronicle with a beautiful, untranslatable name: the *Rajatarangini*, the "River of Kings." Written by the Kashmiri scholar Kalhana around 1150 in a period of political chaos, it surveys the valley's kings from the ancient mists down to his own ruined present. It survives today not as a curiosity but as *the* foundational text of the region's written history — and thanks to M.A. Stein's early-20th-century translation, it sits online in full, free, on the Internet Archive.

## What the book actually is

Kalhana was not a court functionary writing praise; he was writing *against* his own time. His chronicle is explicit that a truthful account must say what happened, out loud, including the bad kings and the disasters — an unusually early and self-aware statement of history as an honest discipline. The poem runs through generations of rulers, wars, conspiracies and ruin with a detached, almost clinical eye. Its completeness is the reason every later scholar of the valley starts from Stein: the text is the spine your sources wrap around.

## The Stein edition, online

The scanned Internet Archive volume is the famously annotated edition — Kalhana's text with Stein's massive apparatus of notes, variant readings, and place-name identifications. For the amateur reader the notes are most of the book's value: they turn an inaccessible poem into a geography and a timeline. The practical way in is to treat the chronicle not as a narrative to be read cover-to-cover but as a *source to be queried* — pick a dynasty, a king, a place, and let Stein's index be your finder.

## Gazetteers: the reference shelf you didn't know you had

For a totally different register of reading, the same shelf holds the imperial gazetteer tradition: geographic dictionaries that describe the region district by district — irrigation, population, revenue, clans. The Gazetteer of Kashmir and Jammu, compiled at the end of the 19th century, and Walter Lawrence's *The Valley of Kashmir* come from this vein, and the Digital South Asia Library keeps the searchable gazetteer tradition alive online. These are *co-ordinate* documents: dry, descriptive, and invaluable for checking what a place was when.

## The two caveats nobody prints on the cover

Primary sources wear their biases on the sleeve, and the honest reader wears them on purpose:

1. **The chronicle is a product of its own politics.** Kalhana wrote after the catastrophe of his own era; his judgments of earlier kings are seasoned by present bitterness. That makes the text *useful evidence* about how 12th-century Kashmiris understood their history — as long as you do not mistake the interpretation for the event.
2. **The gazetteers are products of the empire that commissioned them.** The imperial gaze is part of the data: what the British chose to count was not innocent. You read them for what they record and what they chose to record *about* — both are evidence.

The archive version of these texts is the same encounter every working historian has: **you do not read a document, you interrogate it.** The page is a witness, overconfident in places, blind in others, and indispensable anyway. That is the whole discipline, and the whole pleasure, of primary-source reading as it works from this shelf.
