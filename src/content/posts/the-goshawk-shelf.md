The site is called what it is for a reason, and the reason is not decorative. Northern goshawks (*Accipiter atricapillus*, if you like the current taxonomy) are the largest of the accipiters — the woodland hawks — and watching one work a wood is the closest sane thing to watching an argument with physics. Short, blunt wings and a long tail: built not for altitude but for *geometry*, for threading gaps and whipping through cover at speeds that feel like a mistake.

## What the bird actually is

Listen to All About Birds and the field marks are almost embarrassing: broad white eyebrow, fierce red eye, and a distrustful temperament that the guides describe as "rarely seen" and the falconry literature describes as "seen approaching along the hedge at substantial knots." The goshawk does not stoop from on high like a peregrine. It is a nearer-to-the-ground predator — ambushes from cover, eats tree-roosting prey, and treats woodland density as an advantage rather than an obstacle. The falconry tradition called it the "yard hawk": it hunts the space between the buildings and the trees, which turns out to be exactly where everything worth hunting lives.

## The data habit: eBird

The honest modern instrument is eBird. A species map for the Northern Goshawk is a living object: witness records, seasonal movements, and the quiet fact of range expansions as reintroductions and regrowth happen. For someone who wants to see the bird rather than just eulogize it, the map is the best file. The skill it teaches is the same one as anywhere on this shelf: **the record, kept consistently, is the research.** Observation stacked on observation becomes data; data becomes a claim with a citation. Dead links and dead observations are the same genre of loss.

## The book worth its shelf space

Conor Jameson's *Looking for the Goshawk* spends a year searching for the bird around Britain — part natural history, part biography of an absence, mostly a field notebook done honestly. It is the rare wildlife book that admits to the periods of not seeing anything, and that admission is exactly why it belongs alongside the data: the gap in the record is part of the record. The goshawk is a bird you rediscover, not one you merely find.

## Why a site name, then

Naturalists name projects after their stooping bird because the alternative names — "innovation," "disruption," "synergy" — have no teeth. The goshawk commits. Pick a bird, fly a line. The studio and this site run on the same operating principle as the bird's: when it goes, it goes *properly*, and it holds nothing back on the theory that the quarry might dodge. That is the whole naming story. The wallpaper is just keeping the ledger honest.
