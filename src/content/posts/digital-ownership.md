Most people who "buy" software have never once read the thing they bought. Read the licence agreement and you will find the quiet scandal of the modern age: you do not own the copy — you *rented access* to it, on terms the seller can change, from a store that can go dark, gated by a password that proves nothing except that someone in the chain still holds the key.

## DRM is a euphemism for "you don't own it"

Digital rights management is not protection from criminals. It is protection of the *licence* — the ability of a seller to control what the buyer is allowed to do with a file that physically lives on the buyer's hardware. The EFF has the shortest version: DRM exists to give a store control over goods you have already paid for. It is why a purchased ebook cannot be read on the wrong reader, and why "your copy" can disappear from the shelf when the store's servers shut down.

The kicker that makes it a civil-rights question rather than a consumer-annoyance question: **words de-preserved by DRM are words lost to history.** If a bookseller dies and the DRM server dies with it, the cultural record — paid for, in hand — stops being readable. DeDRM tools exist precisely to convert a licensed purchase into a usable file before the store mutates it into nothing. The tools are controversial only to those who confuse theft with the permanent-preserving of something already paid for.

## Piracy is mostly a complaint about convenience

Every piracy study for the last thirty years comes to the same unfashionable finish: people pay for what is *easy*, and they find workarounds for what is hard. The leading edge is not morality, it is friction — a show unavailable in your country, a game delisted after the store closes, a book printed in a text format no device will open. People do not pirate out of spite; they pirate out of the instinct that a thing they paid for should remain usable. The shadow of that instinct is the fine line this note tiptoes along: preservation is noble, infringement is not, and the industry conflates the two as a matter of policy.

## The practices that cost nothing and buy you decades of autonomy

The alternatives to renting your software are unglamorous and permanent:

- **Prefer the un-DRM'd option.** When a purchase is "yours forever" (which is now a subscription, secretly), read the farther side of the asterisk.
- **Back up what you buy.** Whatever you actually purchase, keep an independent copy on your own hardware. Local-first apps — the ones that store your data in your own files, not a hosted account — are the product category that quietly respects you.
- **Own the machine.** On the device side this is the whole point of the phone-OS projects that ship with an unlocked bootloader and no telemetry default: a device you can re-flash is a device you own. Repair sits in the same family — a right-to-repair ecosystem and parts market are what keep a five-year-old device alive past the store's patience with it.

## The shelf position, stated plainly

The link shelf covers this territory without hosting a single infringing file, and that is the position in one sentence: **the answer to digital ownership is not piracy, it is architecture.** Buy where you can, preserve what you buy, prefer formats and devices you control, and treat every "lifetime guarantee" as a warranty that expires at the first quarterly report that says otherwise.
